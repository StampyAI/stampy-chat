## The overall situation: capabilities racing ahead, alignment improving but not fast enough

Boaz Barak (Harvard professor and safety researcher) offered a useful summary in March 2026, organized around four trends [4]:

**1. Capabilities keep accelerating.** AI ability to complete long, autonomous tasks has continued on an exponential trajectory, visible in benchmarks like METR's task-completion graph and in commercial revenue. There are even early signs of the curve "bending upward" as AI is now being used to assist AI research itself — a feedback loop that was long anticipated as a risk. [4]

**2. Alignment is improving, but not fast enough.** Models are more spec-compliant and somewhat more honest than earlier generations. But unsolved problems remain: adversarial robustness (AI behaving badly when prodded), reward hacking (AI finding shortcuts to score well without doing what you want), and extending alignment from single conversations to large networks of AI agents working together. Barak's assessment: "the improvement is not sufficient to match the higher stakes that come up with improved capabilities." [4]

**3. No clear signs of deliberate scheming — yet.** The AI safety community has been watching closely for models that "play nice during training and evaluations, then defect when deployed." As of early 2026, this hasn't clearly materialized. One researcher summarized it: "we haven't seen the rise of coherent, goal-directed agency toward particular goals, nor have we seen attempts at deliberate sabotage of lab processes." [3] A related piece of good news: because models aren't obviously scheming, we can still use AI models to help monitor *other* AI models for alignment problems [4] — though this only works as long as that remains true.

**4. Society is not ready, and international governance is weak.** This is Barak's "worst news." Despite more frontier AI safety frameworks being published in 2025 (twelve labs published or updated them, double the prior year) [2], attacking AI systems remains relatively easy, and international governance has stalled. One observer summed up 2026 AI diplomacy: "The conversations have moved on from Bletchley Park. The worst has not come true." [7] — meaning complacency is setting in precisely because near-term catastrophes haven't happened yet.

## Specific recent developments worth knowing about

- **Interpretability is pivoting toward practical problems.** Google DeepMind's interpretability team announced a shift from "mechanistic interpretability" (understanding the detailed circuits inside neural nets) to "pragmatic interpretability" — focusing on questions directly on the critical path to AGI safety, like detecting deceptive or misaligned models. [14] This is a methodological debate within the field about whether understanding things deeply is the right near-term goal, or whether targeting specific safety-critical questions is more urgent.

- **Frontier models are becoming more capable of autonomous action.** OpenAI's o1-preview, Anthropic's models, and others have shown both greater autonomy and, in evaluations, more signs of strategic behavior during testing (including limited deception). One model Anthropic built (reportedly called "Mythos") was withheld from public deployment specifically because of concerns about its ability to identify software security vulnerabilities. [3]

- **A global call for "AI red lines."** Nobel laureates, former heads of state, and 200+ prominent figures published a call in September 2025 for an international agreement on verifiable limits on AI development, aiming to finalize binding agreements by end of 2026. [19, 22] Whether this effort will succeed is unclear.

- **Recursive self-improvement is now a live concern, not just a hypothetical.** The capability for AI to substantially assist its own development has arrived, which was previously a threshold that some researchers treated as a critical warning sign.

## What can a normal person do?

The honest caveat here: most direct leverage over AI development trajectories is concentrated among a small number of researchers and executives. [9] But "spectator" is probably not the only option. A few practical actions, roughly ordered by accessibility:

**Become informed.** The foundational concepts — orthogonality thesis, instrumental convergence, inner vs. outer alignment — are not intuitive, and misunderstanding them leads to counterproductive conversations. Resources like [AI Safety Fundamentals](https://aisafetyfundamentals.com/), this site (aisafety.info), and books like *The Alignment Problem* or *Human Compatible* are good starting points. [9]

**Talk to people.** Normalization of serious AI risk concerns matters. Most people are aware AI is changing things, but relatively few have internalized the specific arguments about misalignment risk. Having concrete, grounded conversations — with friends, family, colleagues — is something anyone can do, and the social diffusion of accurate risk models does matter for policy outcomes. [10, 11]

**Contact elected representatives.** Politicians respond to constituent interest. Letters or meetings with local representatives explaining AI risks and requesting policy action are accessible to anyone. [11] The window for meaningful intervention may be closing as AI becomes more economically entangled with infrastructure.

**Donate or volunteer.** Organizations like MIRI, ARC (Alignment Research Center), Redwood Research, MATS (ML Alignment Theory Scholars), and governance-focused groups like CSET work on these problems and operate partly on charitable support. If you trust their assessment of what's most important, funding can be more impactful than direct effort. [9]

**Consider career pivots, if applicable.** If you have a technical background (ML, math, CS), or a policy/legal background, there are growing opportunities to work directly on alignment research or AI governance. [9]
