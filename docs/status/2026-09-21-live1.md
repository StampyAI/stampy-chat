**On warning shots: have any happened?**

Depends heavily on what counts. In the dramatic sense — an AI system escaping, causing mass casualties, prompting a global reckoning — no, nothing clearly fitting that description has happened as of my training knowledge. (I should flag: it's September 2026 and I'm uncertain whether recent events have occurred that I might not know about.)

But researchers like Nate Soares have argued that we've already had things that *should* function as warning shots — and haven't. [28] Zvi Mowshowitz made a similar observation in 2024: "We have already had a number of what should have been 'holy shit' moments. Common sense should already apply, and indeed if you poll people it largely does, but salience of the issue remains low, and politicians so far ignore the will of the people." [6]

The COVID pandemic is the sharpest example. It was an enormous real-world catastrophe — a biological agent disrupting civilization, killing millions, costing trillions — that *should* have galvanized governance around pandemic prevention and gain-of-function research. Soares' 2022 analysis: if we couldn't get global bans on research that creates pandemic-level pathogens "in the wake of a literal pandemic," what makes us think an AI-caused crisis would produce a sane response to AI? The economic forces resisting coordination on biosecurity were "half-a-dozen researchers thinking it's cool." The economic forces behind AI are vastly larger. [28]

There's also a structural problem with the warning-shot theory, pointed out by Daniel Kokotajlo: there may be a very narrow window of capability between an AI that's "too dumb to cause significant damage" and one that's "too smart to fail at takeover if it tried." A system inside that window would presumably also be smart enough to realize it's in that window — and bide its time rather than act visibly. [1] So a warning shot that's dramatic enough to be unambiguous may not arrive before it's too late.

And Joe Carlsmith noted that even if an AI-caused incident does get people paying attention, whether that translates into actually preventing the next, larger-scale version is a separate hard question. [3]

The scenario that worries people most from this cluster: by the time a genuinely unmistakable AI-caused catastrophe happens, "it is too late. Perhaps the weights of a sufficiently advanced model have been irreversibly released." [13] The warning shot paradigm assumes there's a survivable dress rehearsal before the final exam. That assumption may not hold.

---

**On a global moratorium: is it realistic?**

Honest answer: it's extremely politically difficult, but not obviously impossible, and the question of whether it's *realistic* is somewhat separate from whether it's *necessary*.

The MIRI position [TP, 7] is that a worldwide halt on frontier AI development is what the situation *requires*, while acknowledging this is a very large ask. Eliezer Yudkowsky's Time essay made this explicit: "Shut it all down." [7] This isn't offered as a confident prediction that it will happen, but as what honest analysis of the problem implies is needed.

Paul Christiano (a somewhat more moderate safety researcher) put it this way: "If the world were unified around the priority of minimizing global catastrophic risk, I think that we could reduce risk significantly further by implementing a global, long-lasting, and effectively enforced pause... The world is not unified around this goal... and currently seems unlikely to be implemented without much clearer evidence of serious risk." [12]

What would enforcement even look like? The most concrete answer is **compute governance**: controlling access to the high-end chips needed to train frontier models [21]. As the MIRI CEO noted to the US Senate, "Computational power is the main difficult-to-obtain ingredient that has driven the explosive growth in general AI capabilities development." [16] There are proposals involving hardware-level cryptographic controls on advanced AI chips that would require international sign-off to keep running. [23]

The obstacles are real though:
- **Algorithmic progress**: training efficiency improves every year, so the hardware threshold for dangerous AI keeps falling. What requires a supercomputer cluster today may eventually run on consumer hardware. [19]
- **Geopolitical competition**: AI is entangled with national security and economic competitiveness. The US-China dynamic makes coordinated restriction especially fraught, though some Chinese officials have themselves argued for cooperation on safety. [24]
- **"China won't agree" ≠ "don't try"**: Scott Alexander noted that starting treaty negotiations doesn't commit you to signing a bad deal. The objection that other actors won't cooperate isn't itself a reason not to build the infrastructure for coordination. [9]

The COVID analogy cuts in a sobering direction here too: it showed that even with catastrophe already underway, getting coordinated international governance of a dangerous technology is very hard. The forces resisting are large, and the voices for caution have generally been losing ground.
